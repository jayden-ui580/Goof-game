DRAGON WISH: ONLINE - COMBAT DESIGN NOTES
(saved from our combat discussion, for later discussion and eventual implementation)
=====================================================================================

THE IDEA IN ONE BREATH
  The core is block, M1 and dodge. Depth comes from reads (guessing what the other
  person will do), and extra tools ("amplifiers") are optional answers to problems you
  run into as you get better. "Natural Coordination" (live choreography): every hit,
  block and dodge picks a matching animation pair from a big library, so a live fight
  looks like a hand-animated DBZ fight. Same three buttons, endless looks.

  It has a famous precedent: Batman: Arkham's "FreeFlow" combat (three inputs, a huge
  contextual animation library, the attacker nudged so every hit connects).


1. THE CORE IS ALREADY A LOOP
-----------------------------
  After your M1 hits their block, you're stuck in end lag. The defender picks punish or
  side dash; you pick dodge-cancel or stay put (and block as soon as you can).
    - Punish beats staying put .............. (layer 1)
    - Dodge-cancel beats punish ............. (layer 2)
    - Side dash beats dodge-cancel .......... (layer 3)
    - Staying put beats side dash ........... (layer 4: the dash is slow enough that your
                                               end lag is over when they swing, so you
                                               block it)
  Layer 4 closes the loop. Without it, side dash is always right and players find that
  in a week.
  TUNING RULE: a side dash plus a hit must take longer than your end lag.

  Lag note: on Roblox two players see each other about 0.1-0.3 s in the past. This
  system runs on reads, not reactions, which is exactly what survives lag. Keep the
  windows generous.


2. THE HOLE: WHY WOULD ANYONE ATTACK?
-------------------------------------
  If hitting a block is risky and blocking is free, good players wait. Two people
  waiting is the opposite of a DBZ fight. The attacker needs a reward for pressure that
  a beginner gets without learning anything.

  Pick: BLOCKING DRAINS KI. Every blocked hit takes a bit; at zero your guard breaks.
  Mashing (what beginners do anyway) slowly beats a turtle; grabs become the expert's
  faster answer, not the only one.

  The 5 Ki pips on the HUD become the fight's one resource:
    - blocking hits ............. drains slowly; empty = guard break
    - dodge / dodge-cancel / vanish ... 1 pip
    - counter ................... 2 pips
    - burst ..................... 3 pips
    - refill .................... slowly on its own, or fast by CHARGING (the R
                                  power-up), which leaves you wide open. "He's
                                  charging, rush him!" Knocking someone away matters
                                  because distance buys time to charge.
  Free read: out of Ki = can't dodge-cancel = punish without fear. So when you lock on,
  the opponent's plate slides in on the right (mirrored), showing their HP and Ki.


3. RULES FOR AMPLIFIERS (every new mechanic must pass these)
-------------------------------------------------------------
  1. It answers a real problem players hit in playtests (not an imagined one).
  2. A core move beats it (someone who never learned it isn't helpless; just slower).
  3. It costs something, and missing it hurts. No free insurance.
  4. It has its own tell, so the opponent can learn to see it coming.
  5. It fits on 4 buttons: M1, Block, Dodge, Ki. Advanced stuff = two of those pressed
     together, or a direction. (Phones!)
  6. It's taught, not locked. Unlock cosmetics, never tools.

  Starting set:

  Counter and burst share one input: the "panic button" (counter when nobody's hitting
  you, burst while you're being hit).
  The "what beats it" column is mostly core moves: that keeps the game easy to start

  KAIOKEN (added since) fits this list as an amplifier: more power for health. Answer to
  "they out-trade me"; beaten by playing keep-away until the strain eats them.


4. NATURAL COORDINATION (LIVE CHOREOGRAPHY): HOW TO MAKE IT WORK
-----------------------------------------------------------------
  - Moves are the skeleton, animations are skins. Every variant of an M1 has the same
    timing, range, damage and knockback. Variants never decide a fight.
  - Variety within a type, consistency between types. All M1s share one rhythm and one
    wind-up tell (flash + sound); grabs get a different tell (Sekiro flashes a red
    kanji; a red kanji flash fits our HUD). Players can read 4 tells, not 40 animations.
  - The attack picks first, the reaction answers. The attack's variant is chosen when
    the swing starts (nobody knows the outcome yet); the attacker's game tells the
    server which one so every screen agrees. The block / hit / dodge reaction is chosen
    once the outcome is known, to fit that attack.
  - Context picks the family: range (knees and elbows close, punches mid, lunging kicks
    far), air or ground, from behind, opener or finisher, nearby wall or water, form
    (Super Saiyan = flashier). Then choose the variant whose start pose flows from the
    last one's end pose, never the same one twice in a row.
  - Tags make content multiply: attacks are tagged where they land (high/mid/low,
    left/right, light/heavy); reactions are tagged with what they can receive. A new
    attack pairs with every reaction that fits. Mirror every animation for the other
    side. 3 attacks a week then pair with dozens of reactions (3 hand-made pairs a week
    would burn you out in a month).
  - Nudge to connect (Arkham's trick): slide and turn the attacker a little during the
    wind-up so the hit lands on the guard. Locked pairs (grabs, counters) snap into
    place during the impact flash.
  - Anime tricks are lag tricks: wind-ups give the other screen time to catch up;
    hit-stop (a split-second freeze on impact) covers the wait for the server and makes
    hits heavy; vanish teleports hide position corrections.
  - Only take the camera when you've also taken control (locked counters, knock-away
    flights), never during free play.
  - More than two people: reactions only depend on the incoming hit, so they chain from
    different attackers. Locked pairs: a third player's hit breaks the lock and frees
    both.
  - Spend animation time where the camera lingers: finishers, counters, grabs, wall
    slams. M1s are a blur.
  - Bonus 1: the variants ARE the progression. Unlockable, purely cosmetic fighting
    styles; the weekly drop is something players chase, nobody gets an unfair edge.
  - Bonus 2: replays. A fight is a record of which animation played when and where, so
    the game can replay it from cinematic angles: a fight animation made live, ready to
    clip and post.


5. AIR AND KNOCKBACK (ISLAND -> WATER WITHOUT LOSING ANYONE)
------------------------------------------------------------
  - Lock-on camera (the most important piece). Movement is relative to the opponent:
    forward = toward them, left/right circles them. Dashing toward someone in the sky
    takes you into the sky: no up/down controls. Makes 3D readable and flight workable
    on a phone.
  - No free-flight controls in fights (at first). Height changes through clear moments:
    launch (up), spike (down), knock-away (across the map). Flight = travel outside
    fights.
  - The chase mini-game: knocked away, they can vanish-chase (Dodge toward you, 1 pip);
    you can recover early (Dodge). Recover too early and they catch you; vanish after
    you've recovered and they appear next to nothing and get punished.
  - The map reacts through more variants: wall splats, craters, smashing through rocks,
    skipping across the water and splashing in. Zero new controls.
  - Guardrails: only finishers knock away; hard combo limit (4 M1s + finisher; air
    combos end in a finisher); always a way out (recover, burst); the camera follows the
    flight; an arrow points to an off-screen opponent; a soft map edge.


6. KEEP COMBOS SHORT
--------------------
  The DBZ look comes from trades, both fighters doing something. Long combos look like a
  beatdown and make new players quit battlegrounds games. Short combos + real defense =
  more turns = more paired animations = more choreography.


7. WHAT A FIGHT COULD LOOK LIKE
-------------------------------
  Two players lock on at the shore. A mashes M1: a jab snaps off B's forearm guard, then
  a spinning hook B side-blocks. A stops. B swings to punish, but A burns a pip and
  vanishes. B guessed it and side-dashes, catching A with a side kick. Finisher, forward:
  A skips across the water like a stone. B vanish-chases, but A recovers just in time,
  flips upright over the water, and with one pip left starts charging; the aura flares
  and the water ripples. B rushes in. A hits the panic button: the counter catches B's
  fist and turns it into an uppercut. Launch. Now the fight's in the sky.
  Every beat is a decision by one player, and none of it needed more than 4 buttons.


8. BUILD ORDER
--------------
  1. Core: M1 chain, block, dodge(-cancel), side dash + a handful of variants. Prove the
     dream with two players first.
  2. Ki economy (guard drain, pip costs, charging) + opponent's plate on lock-on.
  3. Lock-on, finishers (knock away / launch / spike), chase and recover.
  4. First amplifiers: grab and the panic button.
  5. The map reacting: walls, water, rocks.
  6. Ki blasts, beams, transforming mid-fight.
  Then each week: new variants, and a new amplifier only when playtests show a problem.


9. OPEN QUESTIONS
-----------------
  - 1v1 duels or free-for-all servers? (camera, locked pairs, combo length)
  - How big are ki blasts and beams? (a beam clash is the ultimate paired moment)
  - Transforming mid-fight: the cutscene is ~7 s of standing still. Make it double as a
    burst (the shockwave knocks the opponent away) with a shorter in-fight version?
  - How long should a fight last? (30 s vs 3 min changes HP, Ki, knockback distance)


THE PRINCIPLE UNDERNEATH
------------------------
  Depth = how many real decisions you make per exchange. Complexity = how many rules you
  must learn. The core is high depth, low complexity: every option beats one thing and
  loses to another. Add a mechanic only if it creates decisions for BOTH players (the
  grab gives the defender a new choice: tech, dash back or mash). If every new tool can
  be beaten by an old one, the game never gets harder to start: beginners can always
  play, experts just have better answers.
